Start with why

The Three Ethics

Coined in the 1970s by Australians Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, permaculture rests on three simple ethics. Every design decision gets tested against them.

🌍

Earth Care

Look after soil, water, forests and all living things. In the garden that means building soil instead of depleting it, catching rain, and planting for pollinators and wildlife — not just for ourselves.

🤲

People Care

Design for human needs — fresh food, shade, beauty, rest — without burning out the gardener. A permaculture garden should give you energy, not demand endless weekend labour.

⚖️

Fair Share

Take only what you need and share the surplus: seeds, cuttings, harvests and knowledge. Return excess to the system — compost the scraps, feed the soil, give away the extra courgettes (there are always extra courgettes).

12 Design Principles

Think of these as a checklist for every garden decision — from where to put a bench to how to handle a slug invasion.

1

Observe & Interact

Spend time watching sun, wind, water and wildlife before you dig. "Beauty is in the eye of the beholder."

2

Catch & Store Energy

Harvest rain in barrels, sun in a greenhouse, fertility in compost. Make hay while the sun shines.

3

Obtain a Yield

Design so something useful comes out — food, herbs, flowers, shade. You can't work on an empty stomach.

4

Apply Self-Regulation

Accept feedback. If tomatoes always fail in that corner, the garden is telling you something. Listen.

5

Use Renewable Resources

Compost over fertiliser bags, mulch over plastic, saved seed over bought seed. Let nature do the work.

6

Produce No Waste

Every output is another element's input: kitchen scraps → compost → soil → dinner. A stitch in time saves nine.

7

Design from Patterns

Step back and see the big patterns first — contours, seasons, water flow — then fill in the details.

8

Integrate, Don't Segregate

Mix plants, stack functions, connect elements. Many hands make light work — for plants too.

9

Use Small, Slow Solutions

Start with one bed, not ten. Small changes are easier to maintain and learn from. Slow and steady wins.

10

Use & Value Diversity

Many species = resilience. If one crop fails, others thrive. Don't put all your eggs in one basket.

11

Use Edges & Marginals

The most productive places are where two systems meet — pond edges, path borders, fences, walls.

12

Respond Creatively to Change

Gardens evolve and so does the climate. Treat problems as design prompts, not disasters.

The Six Zones

Place things according to how often you visit them. Herbs you snip daily go by the kitchen door; the wild corner you never touch goes furthest away. Click a zone to explore:

Zone 0 — The Home

You! Your house, your energy, your time. Permaculture design starts with the gardener: a system you can't maintain isn't sustainable, however clever it looks on paper.

Examples: kitchen, seed-saving shelf, garden journal, a comfy chair for observing.

Start in 5 Small Steps

You don't need a master plan or a digger. One season, five moves:

1

Observe for a bit

Note where sun falls, where water pools after rain, which corner is sheltered. Even a week of watching beats guessing. Sketch it roughly.

2

Mulch one bed

Cover soil with cardboard + compost or straw (the "lasagna" below). No digging, no weeding marathon. Plant straight into it.

3

Catch some water

A single rain barrel under a downpipe can save thousands of litres a year — and plants prefer rainwater to tap water.

4

Plant perennials

Rhubarb, herbs, berry bushes, asparagus: plant once, harvest for years. They're the backbone of a low-work garden.

5

Start composting

A simple heap or bin turns kitchen scraps into free fertiliser. Aim for a mix of "greens" (scraps, clippings) and "browns" (leaves, cardboard).

The good stuff

🌿 Tips & Tricks

Fifteen field-tested ideas, each with a gardener's shortcut. Filter by what your garden needs most:

Soil

Never leave soil naked

Bare soil erodes, bakes and loses life. Always cover it — with mulch, ground-cover plants or a green manure like clover or phacelia. Soil under cover stays moist, friable and full of worms.

Trick: Aim for 5–10 cm of straw, shredded leaves or wood chips. Top up little and often, like feeding a pet.
Soil

Go no-dig

Digging destroys fungal networks and brings weed seeds to the surface. Instead, lay compost on top and let worms pull it down. Charles Dowding's trials show equal or better yields with far less work.

Trick: Smother weeds with overlapping cardboard, wet it well, add 10–15 cm of compost, and plant the same day.
Soil

Chop & drop

When a plant finishes or gets unruly, chop it and drop it right where it grew. The biomass feeds soil life in place — no hauling to the compost heap, no nutrients exported.

Trick: Grow comfrey near fruit trees. Cut it 4–5× a season and drop the potassium-rich leaves as mulch around the trunk.
Water

Slow it, spread it, sink it

Rain that runs off is wasted (and takes topsoil with it). Swales — shallow ditches on contour — catch runoff and sink it into the ground where roots can use it for weeks.

Trick: On flat ground, dig "banana circles" or simple mulched basins around trees and direct downpipes into them.
Water

Water deep, not often

A thorough soak once a week grows deep, drought-resistant roots. Daily sprinkling trains shallow roots that panic the moment you go on holiday.

Trick: Bury an unglazed terracotta pot (an olla) beside thirsty plants, fill weekly, and let it seep slowly to the roots.
Water

Greywater & rain chains

Shower and washing-up water (with eco soap) can go on ornamentals and fruit trees. A 100 m² roof in a temperate climate harvests roughly 60,000+ litres a year — mostly free.

Trick: Put a bucket in the shower while it warms up. That's 5 litres a day for your thirstiest pot.
Planting

Plant in guilds, not rows

A guild is a team: a fruit tree + nitrogen-fixers (beans, clover) + nutrient miners (comfrey, dandelion) + ground cover + insect flowers. Each member helps the others.

Trick: Classic apple guild: apple tree, comfrey, chives (deter scab), daffodils (deter voles), clover, and a currant bush.
Planting

Stack in seven layers

A forest grows in layers — canopy, small trees, shrubs, herbs, ground cover, roots and climbers. Copy it and one patch of ground yields seven harvests instead of one.

Trick: Small garden? Do three: a dwarf fruit tree, berry shrubs beneath, strawberries and herbs carpeting the floor.
Planting

Sow little & often

Succession sowing beats the glut-and-famine cycle. A short row of lettuce, radish or beans every two weeks means something is always just ready.

Trick: Let one lettuce and one coriander plant go to seed each year. Free seedlings forever ("volunteers").
Pests & Wildlife

Feed the pest-eaters

Ladybirds, hoverflies, frogs, hedgehogs and birds do your pest control for free. Give them water, undisturbed corners, log piles and flowers from March to October.

Trick: A tiny pond — even a buried washing-up bowl with a ramp — brings frogs that hoover up slugs nightly.
Pests & Wildlife

Trap crops & decoys

Plant something pests love more than your crop, then let them have it. Nasturtiums lure aphids off beans; sacrificial cabbages draw caterpillars away.

Trick: Leave one nettle patch in a corner: it's aphid heaven, which means it's a ladybird restaurant by June.
Pests & Wildlife

The problem is the solution

Too many slugs? You don't have a slug problem — you have a duck (or frog) deficiency. Too many weeds? That's free mulch and a soil report: docks signal compaction, nettles signal rich soil.

Trick: Beer traps work, but a plank on the soil works better — slugs hide under it by day, ready for the birds.
Time & Energy

Every element, 3 jobs

Before adding anything, ask: what three functions can it serve? A hedge can be a windbreak + wildlife corridor + edible berries. A pond: irrigation + frog habitat + light reflector.

Trick: Train a grapevine over a sunny pergola — summer shade, autumn fruit, winter sun through bare vines.
Time & Energy

Perennials do the overtime

Annual veg needs sowing every year; perennials just show up. Asparagus, artichokes, sorrel, tree onions, rhubarb and soft fruit give harvests for a decade from one planting.

Trick: Perennial kale (Daubenton's) lives 5+ years and propagates from side-shoots stuck straight in the soil.
Time & Energy

Keep a 2-minute journal

Note sowing dates, first harvests, what flopped. Next year you'll garden with data instead of déjà vu. Photos from the same spot each month are gold.

Trick: Write the sow date on the seed packet and file packets in an old shoebox by month. Instant sowing calendar.

Recipe of the garden

Build a No-Dig "Lasagna" Bed

The fastest way to turn lawn or weedy ground into a fertile bed — no digging, plant the same weekend. Build layers bottom to top:

🌿 Mulch — straw or leaf mould 5 cm · keep moist
🟫 Compost / topsoil 10–15 cm · plant into this
🍃 Greens — grass clippings, comfrey 5 cm · nitrogen
🍂 Browns — straw, shredded leaves 10 cm · carbon
💩 Aged manure (optional) 3–5 cm · rocket fuel
📰 Cardboard / newspaper overlapped, soaked, tape removed
🌱 Existing lawn or weeds mow short — don't dig!

Water each layer as you go. The cardboard smothers weeds and rots away within a season while worms do the mixing.

💡 Lasagna golden rules

1. Wet everything. Each layer should be as damp as a wrung-out sponge — dry layers can repel water for months.

2. No gaps in the cardboard. Couch grass and bindweed need only a keyhole of light. Overlap edges by 15 cm.

3. Plant smart on day one. Transplants (courgettes, beans, potatoes pushed into the mulch) thrive immediately; fine seeds do better after the layers settle for a few weeks.

4. Expect shrinkage. A 40 cm lasagna becomes 15 cm of rich soil by autumn. That's not failure — that's composting in place. Top up yearly.

5. Edges matter. Mow or barrier the perimeter so lawn grass doesn't creep back in from the sides.

Avoid the Classic Mistakes

…and tick off your starter wins as you go.

❌ Doing everything in year one

Earthworks, 20 beds and a food forest at once = burnout by August. Principle 9: small and slow. Master one area, then expand.

❌ Planting before observing

That "perfect" herb spot may be a frost pocket or winter pond. A few weeks of watching (or one rainy-day walk) saves a season of disappointment.

❌ Importing fertility forever

Buying compost to start is fine; depending on it forever isn't a system. Grow your own fertility: comfrey, clover, compost, leaf mould.

❌ Fighting for tidiness

Neat, bare, sprayed gardens are deserts. A little mess — seed heads standing, logs rotting, leaves lying — is habitat and free pest control.

❌ Copying designs from other climates

Swales are magic in drylands; in a rainy Dutch polder you may need drainage and raised beds instead. Design for your water, soil and seasons.

Curious minds

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the questions every new permaculturist asks.

Is permaculture just organic gardening?

Organic gardening avoids chemicals; permaculture goes further — it's a design system. It plans where things go (zones), how they connect (guilds), and how energy and water flow, so the garden increasingly runs itself. A permaculture garden is organic, but an organic garden isn't automatically permaculture.

I only have a balcony. Can I still do this?

Absolutely. Zones shrink to metres (herbs by the door = Zone 1), stacking becomes vertical (climbers up the railing), a worm bin replaces the compost heap, and a bucket catches rain. Micro-permaculture is real permaculture.

Does permaculture mean letting the garden go wild?

No — it means designed abundance. Zone 5 is deliberately wild, but Zones 1–2 can look neat and highly productive. It's managed like a forest, not abandoned like a vacant lot. You guide succession instead of fighting it.

How long until it "takes care of itself"?

Honest answer: it gets easier every year, but no food garden is zero-work. Expect front-loaded effort in years 1–2 (beds, planting perennials, mulching), then a shift from building to harvesting and tweaking as soil life, perennials and predators take over the chores.

What's the single highest-impact first step?

Mulch. Covering bare soil simultaneously conserves water, suppresses weeds, feeds soil life, moderates temperature and builds fertility — five functions from one action. If you do nothing else this page suggests, do that.

Which books should I read next?

Great starters: Gaia's Garden by Toby Hemenway (home-scale, very practical), Permaculture: Principles & Pathways Beyond Sustainability by David Holmgren (the philosophy), The Vegetable Gardener's Guide to Permaculture by Christopher Shein, and Charles Dowding's no-dig books for the veg patch. Your own garden journal will become the most relevant book of all.
